# Practice questions

1. What dilemma is captured by the Prisoner's Dilemma? Distinguish individual from collective rationality.
2. Consider the symmetric payoff orderings (a) $T>R>P>S$, (b) $R>T>P>S$ and (c) $T>R>S>P$. Which defines a Prisoner's Dilemma? Explain using the incentives to cooperate or defect rather than a story attached to the game.
3. Extend the Prisoner's Dilemma to three players. Specify actions, payoffs and any dominant strategy.
4. Analyse either Stag Hunt or Chicken: define its scenario, payoff ordering and equilibrium structure.
5. Write pseudocode for an evolutionary iterated-Prisoner's-Dilemma tournament in which strategies are inherited, payoff determines fitness and mutation is possible. State how success will be measured.
6. Distinguish one-shot play, repeated play, tournament success and evolutionary success. Why can the same strategy rank differently under each?
7. Explain why Tit for Tat performed well in Axelrod's tournaments. Give an environment or error process under which it may cease to do so.
8. Translate payoff into reproductive fitness in two different ways. How can that modelling choice alter the evolutionary outcome?
9. Rock–paper–scissors is a game in the game-theoretic sense. Write its payoff matrix, then explain what new assumptions are introduced when many spatial agents reproduce or replace neighbours.
10. Compare well-mixed and networked populations. Which model details determine who interacts, who imitates and how strategies spread?
11. Design an ensemble experiment comparing two update rules while controlling the payoff matrix, population, network and mutation rate.
